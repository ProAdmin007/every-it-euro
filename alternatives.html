What do we mean by a “European technology company”?

An office in Europe is not enough. We look at who controls the company and where its capability sits.

Criteria we consider

  • Headquarters — where the operating company is registered and managed.
  • Ultimate parent company — the top of the ownership chain.
  • Ownership and control — founders, listed shareholders, private equity, state. A non-European controlling shareholder changes the picture.
  • R&D location — where the product is primarily engineered.
  • Intellectual property — which entity owns the code and patents.
  • Operational control — who runs the services, update channels and support.
  • Infrastructure — whose data centres and cloud platforms are used.
  • Jurisdiction — which laws can ultimately compel the company.

We verify headquarters and ownership from official company sources and reputable reporting. R&D location, IP ownership and infrastructure are noted where public information allows, and are often not disclosed in detail. Ask suppliers directly — the checklist helps.

EU/EEA headquartered

Headquarters and ultimate control in an EU or EEA country. Includes companies listed on European exchanges whose control is not concentrated outside Europe.

European headquartered (non-EU)

Headquarters and control in a European country outside the EU/EEA, such as Switzerland or the United Kingdom. European, but under a different legal framework from the EU.

Part of a European group

The operating company belongs to a larger European group — for example a cloud provider owned by a European retailer or telecom group.

European open-source project

Primarily developed in Europe under an open-source licence, often by a foundation or a public-sector initiative. Usable and forkable by anyone.

Mixed / international ownership

European roots or operations, but meaningful non-European ownership, control or headquarters — for example a non-European private-equity owner or holding company. Listed for transparency, because ownership matters.

European ownership is not the same as independence

A European company may build on non-European cloud platforms, chips, operating systems or open-source libraries maintained elsewhere, and it may have international investors. Classification describes control, not complete technological independence — which almost no company anywhere has.

Ownership changes to watch

European technology companies are regularly acquired by non-European owners. That is a normal part of a global market, and it can benefit founders and investors. It also shows why “who owns the supplier?” deserves a recurring place in your risk assessment, not a one-off check.
